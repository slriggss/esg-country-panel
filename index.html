Two decades of Environmental, Social, and Governance data, 217 countries at a time

Shelby L. Riggs · Financial Data Analytics · Data Website Project

This report reads 22 years (2002–2023) of country-level World Bank data through an ESG lens — pairing environmental indicators (energy mix, emissions, forest cover), social indicators (inequality, health, labor), and governance indicators (rule of law, regulatory quality, corruption control) for the same 217 countries, year over year. The goal isn't a single ESG score; it's testing whether the relationships an ESG analyst assumes — that better governance tracks with better outcomes, that the energy transition is visibly underway — actually show up in two decades of data.

Environmental

The average country is no more renewable-powered than it was in 2002

Averaged across every reporting country, renewable energy's share of final energy consumption actually slipped from 31.2% in 2002 to 29.1% in 2021 — a 2.1 point decline, not the steady climb the "energy transition" narrative implies. This is a simple average of each country's own share, so it isn't dominated by a few large economies; it says the typical country hasn't shifted its energy mix toward renewables over two decades. Solar and wind headlines are real, but they're concentrated in a handful of large, fast-moving markets — not broad-based across the 170+ countries this average covers each year.

World Bank indicator EG.FEC.RNEW.ZS, unweighted mean across reporting countries. 2022–2023 excluded: only ~35% of the usual country panel had reported yet at data-pull time (see methodology).

Social

Electricity access still splits almost exactly along income lines

In 2023, high-income countries averaged 99.9% electricity access and upper-middle-income countries 97.6% — both essentially universal. Lower-middle-income countries averaged 79.0%, and low-income countries just 43.5%: a 56.4-point gap between the richest and poorest income tier. This isn't a story of gradual convergence so much as two different worlds — access is a solved problem for three of the four income tiers and an open one for the fourth.

World Bank indicator EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS, mean by World Bank income classification, 2002–2023.

Environmental

High-income countries emit more than seven times the CO2 per person of low-income countries

In 2023, the average high-income country produced 11.2 tonnes of CO2-equivalent per person, versus 1.55 tonnes in the average low-income country — a 7.2x ratio. Upper-middle-income countries (5.65 t) and lower-middle-income countries (2.36 t) fall in between, roughly stepping down with income. Read alongside the electricity-access finding above, the pattern is uncomfortable for ESG framing that treats "emissions" and "development" as separable: the countries furthest from full electricity access are also the ones with the smallest carbon footprint per person.

World Bank indicator EN.GHG.ALL.PC.CE.AR5 (total GHG excluding land-use change), mean by income group, 2023.

Environmental

Coal's share of the electricity mix has drifted down, but slowly

The average country generated 12.5% of its electricity from coal in 2002, falling to 10.7% by 2021 — a real but modest 1.85-point decline over two decades. That's a much smaller move than the renewable-share finding above would predict if coal were being displaced directly by renewables; more likely, natural gas and other sources are absorbing some of coal's lost share too.

World Bank indicator EG.ELC.COAL.ZS, unweighted mean across reporting countries. 2022–2023 excluded for the same thin-coverage reason as the renewables chart.

Governance

Government effectiveness is one of the strongest predictors of electricity access

Across 4,370 country-year observations, the correlation between the World Bank's Government Effectiveness score and electricity access is r = 0.574 — a strong positive relationship for cross-country social data. Each dot below is one country's 2002–2023 average on both measures. Governance quality doesn't guarantee electricity access, but almost no country with weak governance scores also has strong electricity access — the bottom-left of the chart is dense; the bottom-right is nearly empty.

World Bank indicators GOV_WGI_GE.EST and EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS. Each point is one country's own multi-year average (203 countries with data on both).

Social

Latin America remains the world's most unequal region by income

Among regions with 2023 Gini data, Latin America & the Caribbean tops the list at 44.8, followed by North America at 41.8 and Sub-Saharan Africa at 39.4. Europe & Central Asia sits lowest, at 31.3. The ranking has been broadly stable for decades in the development-economics literature; this panel confirms it's still true through the most recent reporting year.

World Bank indicator SI.POV.GINI, mean by region, most recent year each region reported in 2023. South Asia excluded: no country in that region reported a 2023 Gini figure.

Governance

Women's parliamentary representation has nearly doubled since 2002

The average share of parliamentary seats held by women rose from 13.6% in 2002 to 25.5% in 2023 — an 11.9-point gain. It's the clearest steady, broad-based trend in this entire dataset: unlike the renewable energy and coal figures above, this one moved in the same direction almost every year of the panel.

World Bank indicator SG.GEN.PARL.ZS, unweighted mean across reporting countries, 2002–2023.

Governance

Stronger regulatory quality tracks with cleaner air — a moderate but real relationship

Regulatory Quality and PM2.5 air pollution correlate at r = −0.333 across 4,294 country-year observations (n = 199 countries by multi-year average) — weaker than the governance/electricity relationship above, but still a meaningful negative relationship: better-regulated countries tend to have less fine-particulate air pollution, even though regulation is far from the only thing driving air quality (geography, industrial mix, and population density all matter too).

World Bank indicators GOV_WGI_RQ.EST and EN.ATM.PM25.MC.M3. Each point is one country's own multi-year average.

Composite

Building an ESG composite score from this data — and where it lands 207 countries

Every other finding above looks at one indicator at a time. This one combines all 24: for each of the 207 countries with data in every pillar, each indicator-year is converted to a percentile rank against every other country that reported it (0–100), indicators where a high value is a bad outcome are inverted so 100 always means the best outcome, and the result is averaged into an Environmental score, a Social score, and a Governance score, then averaged once more into a single composite. It's a transparent, equally-weighted score — not a claim that this is *the* right way to weight ESG, but a demonstration of how one would build it from a panel like this, and a sanity check on the rest of the report: small, wealthy, stable democracies (Liechtenstein, the Nordics, Switzerland) top the list, and states in active conflict or state collapse (Somalia, Yemen, Afghanistan, Syria) sit at the bottom — which is exactly what a face-valid ESG score should show.

Composite = average of Environmental, Social, and Governance pillar scores, each the mean percentile rank across that pillar's indicators and all years 2002–2023. Full method in the closing section below.

About this data set

The data comes from the World Bank's API, combining its purpose-built Environment, Social and Governance (ESG) Data collection with the Worldwide Governance Indicators, pulled programmatically (see scripts/fetch_data.js) rather than downloaded by hand.

What one row is. One row is one country, in one year, for one indicator — for example, Brazil's forest area in 2015. The full panel (data/esg_panel.csv) has 95,355 such rows: 217 countries × 2002–2023 × 24 indicators, minus the combinations no country reported.

Indicators tracked. 9 environmental (electricity access, renewable energy and electricity share, coal share, fossil fuel share, forest area, greenhouse gas emissions per capita, PM2.5 air pollution, protected land/marine area), 8 social (unemployment, life expectancy, income inequality, safe drinking water, primary school completion, under-5 mortality, labor force participation, hospital beds), and 7 governance (the six Worldwide Governance Indicators — control of corruption, government effectiveness, rule of law, regulatory quality, voice and accountability, political stability — plus women's parliamentary representation).

Rows dropped, and why. The World Bank API returns a row for every country/year/indicator combination whether or not a value was actually reported; rows with no reported value are dropped entirely rather than filled in, so every remaining row is a real reported figure. Two charts above (renewable energy and coal share) additionally exclude 2022–2023: those years had only 35–45% of the usual country count reported at data-pull time, which would make the "latest year" average reflect a handful of fast-reporting countries rather than a global figure. The dashboard lets you inspect this yourself — filter to those indicators and years to see the drop in country count directly.

How every rate and average is computed. Every "average" above is an unweighted mean across the countries that reported a value for that indicator in that year or year range — not weighted by population or GDP, so it reflects the typical country rather than the typical person. Every correlation is a Pearson correlation coefficient computed on the full set of overlapping country-year observations, with 90/10 confidence-interval scatter points instead plotted as one country-level average (across all years) per country, to keep the chart legible. Percentile ranks used on the dashboard rank each country against every other country reporting the same indicator in the same year (0 = lowest, 100 = highest). Year-over-year percent change is (this year − last year) / |last year| for the same country and indicator. The composite ESG score averages each country's percentile rank across every indicator it has data for, within a pillar, across all years, then averages the three pillar scores; indicators where a high value is a bad outcome (coal share, fossil fuel share, GHG per capita, PM2.5, unemployment, Gini, under-5 mortality) are inverted first. A country is only scored if it has at least three data points in each of the three pillars. All of this is computed in scripts/analyze.js, which reads directly from the same CSV the dashboard loads — nothing here is hand-typed.
