Shelby L. Riggs · Financial Data Analytics · Data Website Project

This report reads {{head.nyears}} years ({{head.years}}) of country-level World Bank data through an ESG lens — pairing environmental indicators (energy mix, emissions, forest cover), social indicators (inequality, health, labor), and governance indicators (rule of law, regulatory quality, corruption control) for the same {{head.countries}} countries, year over year. The goal isn't a single ESG score; it's testing whether the relationships an ESG analyst assumes — that better governance tracks with better outcomes, that the energy transition is visibly underway — actually show up in two decades of data.

Environmental

Renewables are gaining in electricity, but the average country's total energy mix hasn't gotten greener

Averaged across every reporting country, renewable energy's share of total final energy consumption slipped from {{ren.first}}% in {{ren.y0}} to {{ren.last}}% in {{ren.y1}} — a {{ren.drop}}-point decline. This is a simple average of each country's own share, so every country counts equally. But the World Bank counts traditional biomass (firewood, charcoal) as renewable, and the decline sits in the poorest countries: lower-middle-income countries fell from {{ren.lmi.first}}% to {{ren.lmi.last}}% and low-income countries from {{ren.li.first}}% to {{ren.li.last}}%, while high-income countries rose from {{ren.hi.first}}% to {{ren.hi.last}}%. That pattern most likely reflects households moving off traditional biomass as they gain modern energy, rather than a retreat from modern renewables. (The set of reporting countries changes from year to year, so as a check: among the {{ren.same.n}} countries with data in both {{ren.y0}} and {{ren.y1}}, the average moved from {{ren.same.first}}% to {{ren.same.last}}%, a {{ren.same.drop}}-point decline — the trend isn't an artifact of who reported.)

Electricity tells a different story. Among the {{ren.elec.n}} countries that reported in both {{ren.y0}} and {{ren.y1}}, the average renewable share of electricity generation rose from {{ren.elec.first}}% to {{ren.elec.last}}%, and it rose in {{ren.elec.rose}} of them. The power-sector transition is broad-based; it just hasn't yet moved countries' total energy mix, which still includes heating, transport, and industry.

World Bank indicator EG.FEC.RNEW.ZS (includes traditional biomass), unweighted mean across reporting countries; income-group and electricity figures (EG.ELC.RNEW.ZS) in the text are computed in scripts/analyze.js. 2022–2023 excluded: only ~35% of the usual country panel had reported yet at data-pull time (see methodology).

Social

Electricity access still splits almost exactly along income lines

In {{acc.year}}, high-income countries averaged {{acc.hi}}% electricity access and upper-middle-income countries {{acc.umi}}% — both essentially universal. Lower-middle-income countries averaged {{acc.lmi}}%, and low-income countries just {{acc.li}}%: a {{acc.gap}}-point gap between the richest and poorest income tier. This isn't a story of gradual convergence so much as two different worlds — access is a solved problem for three of the four income tiers and an open one for the fourth.

Environmental

High-income countries emit more than seven times the CO2 per person of low-income countries

In {{ghg.year}}, emissions per person stepped down with income: the average high-income country produced {{ghg.ratio}} times the CO2-equivalent per person of the average low-income country. Read alongside the electricity-access finding above, the pattern is uncomfortable for ESG framing that treats "emissions" and "development" as separable: the countries furthest from full electricity access are also the ones with the smallest carbon footprint per person.

  • High income — {{ghg.hi}} t per person. The largest footprint of the four tiers, {{ghg.ratio}}× the low-income average.
  • Upper middle income — {{ghg.umi}} t. Roughly half the high-income level.
  • Lower middle income — {{ghg.lmi}} t. About a fifth of the high-income level.
  • Low income — {{ghg.li}} t. The smallest footprint — and, from the finding above, the tier with the lowest electricity access ({{acc.li}}%).

World Bank indicator EN.GHG.ALL.PC.CE.AR5 (total GHG excluding land-use change), mean by income group, {{ghg.year}}.

Environmental

Coal's share of the electricity mix has drifted down, but slowly

The average country generated {{coal.first}}% of its electricity from coal in {{coal.y0}}, falling to {{coal.last}}% by {{coal.y1}} — a real but modest {{coal.drop}}-point decline over two decades. Holding the country set fixed gives the same picture: the {{coal.same.n}} countries with data in both years went from {{coal.same.first}}% to {{coal.same.last}}%. That's a much smaller move than the renewable-share finding above would predict if coal were being displaced directly by renewables; more likely, natural gas and other sources are absorbing some of coal's lost share too.

World Bank indicator EG.ELC.COAL.ZS, unweighted mean across reporting countries. 2022–2023 excluded for the same thin-coverage reason as the renewables chart.

Governance

Effective government and electricity access go together — but the link runs one way

Across {{gov.n}} country-year observations, the correlation between the World Bank's Government Effectiveness score and electricity access is r = {{gov.r}}. On the {{gov.nc}} country averages plotted below (each dot is one country's {{head.years}} average on both measures), r = {{gov.rc}} — a strong positive relationship for cross-country social data. It is an association, not a demonstration that governance causes access: income, geography, and governance move together, and the country-years behind the first figure are not independent observations.

The pattern is lopsided. Of the {{gov.strong}} countries with a governance score at or above zero, only {{gov.strong80}} average below 80% electricity access — the bottom-right of the chart is nearly empty. But weak governance is not a barrier on its own: {{gov.weak90}} of the {{gov.weak}} countries scoring below zero average above 90%. Good governance looks close to enough for near-universal access; its absence doesn't rule it out.

World Bank indicators GOV_WGI_GE.EST and EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS. Each point is one country's own multi-year average ({{gov.nc}} countries with data on both). Dashed line: least-squares fit to the plotted country averages; the r shown on the chart is for those same points.

Social

Latin America & the Caribbean has had the highest income inequality of any region in every year of the panel

Gini data is reported unevenly, so each country counts once, at its most recent reading between {{gini.from}} and {{gini.to}} ({{gini.n}} countries in all). On that basis {{gini.r1.name}} averages {{gini.r1.avg}} ({{gini.r1.n}} countries), followed by {{gini.r2.name}} at {{gini.r2.avg}} ({{gini.r2.n}} countries). {{gini.low.name}} sits lowest at {{gini.low.avg}} ({{gini.low.n}} countries). North America shows {{gini.na.avg}}, but that rests on only {{gini.na.n}} countries, so it shouldn't be read as a regional pattern. The top spot isn't a fluke of the window: comparing whichever countries reported in each year, {{gini.r1.name}} had the highest regional average in {{gini.topyears}} of {{gini.years}} years.

Governance

Stronger regulatory quality tracks with cleaner air — a moderate but real relationship

Regulatory Quality and PM2.5 air pollution correlate at r = {{reg.r}} across {{reg.n}} country-year observations, and at r = {{reg.rc}} across the {{reg.nc}} country averages plotted below — weaker than the governance/electricity relationship above, but still a meaningful negative relationship: better-regulated countries tend to have less fine-particulate air pollution, even though regulation is far from the only thing driving air quality (geography, industrial mix, and population density all matter too). As above, this is an association rather than proof that regulation causes cleaner air.

World Bank indicators GOV_WGI_RQ.EST and EN.ATM.PM25.MC.M3. Each point is one country's own multi-year average. Dashed line: least-squares fit to the plotted country averages; the r shown on the chart is for those same points.

Composite

Building an ESG composite score from this data — and where it lands {{esg.n}} countries

Every other finding above looks at one indicator at a time. This one combines all {{esg.total}}. Each country's value on each indicator is converted to a percentile rank against every other country that reported it that year (0–100); indicators where a high value is a bad outcome are inverted so 100 always means the best outcome. A country's years are averaged within each indicator, the indicators are averaged into an Environmental, a Social, and a Governance score, and those three are averaged into the composite. Every indicator carries equal weight within its pillar and every pillar carries equal weight overall — a transparent choice, not a claim that this is the right way to weight ESG.

Countries that report only a handful of indicators can't be ranked fairly, because their score depends on which few happen to be available. So a country is scored only if it reports at least {{esg.min}} of the {{esg.total}} indicators and at least two in each pillar; that leaves {{esg.n}} countries and sets aside {{esg.excluded}}, mostly small economies and territories. As a sanity check, {{esg.top5}} lead the list and {{esg.bottom5}} sit at the bottom — a mix of wealthy, stable countries at the top and conflict-affected or fragile states at the bottom, which is what a face-valid score should show. Two cautions: the top five sit within {{esg.top5.spread}} points of each other, so their exact order means little; and the environmental pillar is where even leaders lose ground (for {{esg.top1.name}}, {{esg.top1.env}} on Environmental against {{esg.top1.gov}} on Governance), because wealthy countries do poorly on per-capita emissions and fossil-fuel reliance.

About this data set

The data comes from the World Bank's API, combining its purpose-built Environment, Social and Governance (ESG) Data collection with the Worldwide Governance Indicators, pulled programmatically (see scripts/fetch_data.js) rather than downloaded by hand.

What one row is. One row is one country, in one year, for one indicator — for example, Brazil's forest area in 2015. The full panel (data/esg_panel.csv) has {{head.rows}} such rows: {{head.countries}} countries × {{head.years}} × {{head.indicators}} indicators, minus the combinations no country reported.

Indicators tracked. 9 environmental (electricity access, renewable energy and electricity share, coal share, fossil fuel share, forest area, greenhouse gas emissions per capita, PM2.5 air pollution, protected land/marine area), 8 social (unemployment, life expectancy, income inequality, safe drinking water, primary school completion, under-5 mortality, labor force participation, hospital beds), and 7 governance (the six Worldwide Governance Indicators — control of corruption, government effectiveness, rule of law, regulatory quality, voice and accountability, political stability — plus women's parliamentary representation).

Rows dropped, and why. The World Bank API returns a row for every country/year/indicator combination whether or not a value was actually reported; rows with no reported value are dropped entirely rather than filled in, so every remaining row is a real reported figure. Two charts above (renewable energy and coal share) additionally exclude 2022–2023: those years had only 35–45% of the usual country count reported at data-pull time, which would make the "latest year" average reflect a handful of fast-reporting countries rather than a global figure. The dashboard lets you inspect this yourself — filter to those indicators and years to see the drop in country count directly.

Averages. Every "average" above is an unweighted mean across the countries that reported a value for that indicator in that year or year range — not weighted by population or GDP, so it reflects the typical country rather than the typical person. Because the set of reporting countries changes over time, the trend findings also report the change for a fixed set of countries (those with data in both the first and last year), and the renewable-electricity comparison uses only countries that reported in both years.

Correlations. Every correlation is a Pearson coefficient. The report gives two versions: one on all overlapping country-year observations, and one on the country-level averages (one point per country, across all years) that the scatter plots show. The dashed line on each scatter is fit to those country averages, and the r printed on the chart is computed on the same points. Correlations describe association only.

Percentiles and change. Percentile ranks on the dashboard rank each country against every other country reporting the same indicator in the same year (0 = lowest, 100 = highest). Year-over-year percent change is (this year − last year) / |last year| for the same country and indicator.

Regional inequality. Each country counts once in the Gini comparison, at its most recent value from {{gini.from}} to {{gini.to}}, because Gini surveys are irregular and a single year leaves some regions with one or two countries. The number of countries behind each region is shown on the chart.

The composite score. For each indicator, a country's percentile rank in each year is averaged across its years; indicators where a high value is a bad outcome (coal share, fossil fuel share, GHG per capita, PM2.5, unemployment, Gini, under-5 mortality) are inverted first. Indicator scores are averaged into three pillar scores, and the pillar scores are averaged into the composite, so each indicator has equal weight inside its pillar regardless of how many years it reports. A country is scored only if it reports at least {{esg.min}} of the {{esg.total}} indicators and at least two in each pillar. The score measures relative standing among countries that report, not absolute performance, and it depends on the equal-weight choice.

Reproducibility. Every figure and every number in the written findings is computed in scripts/analyze.js from the same CSV the dashboard loads, and the page text is filled in from that output by scripts/build_report.js, which also checks the report's verbal claims (for example, "rose in every year") against the data before it will build.

Data: World Bank ESG Data & Worldwide Governance Indicators, pulled via the World Bank API. Built with Claude Code.
